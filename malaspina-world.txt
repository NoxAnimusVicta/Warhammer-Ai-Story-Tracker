Malaspina is an isolated human death world of ancient industrial cities, fortified aristocratic estates, and immense wildernesses. Its inhabitants live beneath the recurring threat of technologically superior xenos who use the planet as a hunting preserve. Human civilisation endures through heavy industry, disciplined military service, locally maintained technology, and jealously guarded traditions of psychic practice.

Founded by human settlers during the Dark Age of Technology, Malaspina lost interstellar contact during the Age of Strife. It was never brought into Imperial Compliance and has no surviving Imperial administration, Mechanicus establishment, Ecclesiarchy or recognisable Imperial creed. Its founding civilisation collapsed through isolation and repeated local catastrophes; present societies rebuilt unevenly from the remains.

Three principal continents divide its inhabited surface: Otranto, Morholt, and Vesalius. Each supports distinct human societies shaped by its terrain, resources, and exposure to the hunters. No government commands the entire planet or any of its three principal continents. Authority belongs to competing city-states, hereditary houses, military strongholds, and industrial oligarchies whose territories overlap through treaties, marriages, debts, and longstanding claims.

Otranto holds the greatest concentration of population and industry. Jagged mountain chains enclose broad agricultural basins, while rivers connect densely settled valleys. Its cities climb fortified terraces: ivory palaces and copper domes overlook manufactoria, workers’ districts, rail terminals, and smoking foundries. Monumental aqueducts cross crowded avenues. Armoured trains pass through mountain tunnels and over immense viaducts, carrying grain, ore, troops, and machinery between defended settlements.

Otranto’s cultivated districts depend upon extensive drainage works, pumping stations, reservoirs, and maintained perimeter defences. Beyond these enclosures, forests advance rapidly across cleared ground. Agricultural wealth confers considerable political power, and disputes over water, transport routes, and food exports are frequent causes of conflict.

Morholt is colder, more heavily forested, and more sparsely settled. Towering conifers cover deep valleys beneath glacial mountains. Snowbound fortress cities surround coal-fired generating stations, district-heating plants, mineral workings, and sheltered industrial sites. Heated glasshouses provide food within their walls. Tracked vehicles and armoured supply trains connect the larger settlements, while distant outposts depend on seasonal expeditions.

The wilderness of Morholt contains extensive remnants of earlier human occupation. Marble colonnades stand among enormous roots; collapsed transit lines disappear beneath ferns and luminous fungi. Some ruins contain corroded boilers, seized pumps and collapsed industrial workings from earlier local settlements. Any surviving fragments of the original interstellar colony are exceptionally rare, broken and effectively unrecognisable. Others have become dens for native predators or sites frequented by the alien hunters. Local troops specialise in reconnaissance, tracking, survival, and combat beneath the forest canopy.

Vesalius is a continent of volcanic uplands, dense jungles, and violent coastal weather. Steaming rivers descend through black basalt terraces. Offshore stone pillars and rugged cliffs shelter deep natural harbours. Human settlement concentrates along the coasts, where fortified ports support shipyards, fisheries, mineral exports, and maritime trade.

Vesalian cities rise in crowded terraces above their waterfronts. Copper roofs, stone mansions, furnace chimneys, and bronze navigation beacons overlook drydocks and armoured cargo vessels. Inland, flowering vegetation covers abandoned airfields, overgrown terraces and the weathered foundations of forgotten settlements. Expeditions seek mineral deposits and recoverable conventional machinery, supported by small settlements whose survival depends on regular coastal supply.

Malaspina’s ecology makes permanent habitation an undertaking requiring constant labour. Large predators stalk livestock, patrols, and industrial clearings. Some possess heavy natural plating; others hunt through heightened sensitivity to vibration, heat, or scent. Seasonal fungal blooms threaten stored food and exposed lungs. Aggressive vegetation invades roads, drainage channels, and foundations. Waterborne parasites require reliable treatment and filtration.

These hazards sustain entire professions. Perimeter crews clear vegetation, military engineers maintain transport corridors, and specialist workers inspect filters, reservoirs, and food stores. A settlement’s security depends as much on functioning machinery and dependable supplies as on its weapons.

Human culture combines elaborate courtly traditions with industrial militarism. Noble households maintain formal uniforms, heraldic devices, genealogical records, and private military forces. Officers wear high collars, long coats, polished armour, and carefully displayed service distinctions. Ivory, deep green, bronze, and gold feature prominently among Otranto’s wealthy households, while regional forces adopt clothing suited to their environments.

Prestige also belongs to skilled artificers, engineers, physicians, and scholars. Patronage of a foundry, academy, hospital, or public conservatory demonstrates wealth and authority. Art, music, ornamental craftsmanship, and monumental architecture flourish wherever resources permit. Beneath that splendour, much of the population works in demanding conditions, bound to employers and ruling families through inherited obligations, debt, or dependence upon protected accommodation.

Human technology is unevenly distributed, concentrated in defended industrial centres and their military arsenals. Factories produce steel, machine tools, electrical equipment, industrial chemicals, combustion engines and conventional armoured vehicles. Coal and oil power thermal stations, while suitable regions exploit hydroelectricity. Wired telephones, telegraph networks and radio support communications.

A wealthy household can equip its retainers with well-made rifles, submachine guns, machine guns, mortars and artillery, supported by tanks, armoured cars and military trucks. Poorer provincial levies rely on older firearms and limited ammunition. Propeller-driven aircraft operate from defended airfields, and steel steamships and diesel vessels serve the coasts. Access to fuel, replacement components, trained personnel and transport determines military strength. Native humanity has no working spacecraft or orbital infrastructure. Exceptionally rare ancient human offworld debris is broken and effectively unrecognisable.

Rare captured hunter weapons and devices circulate as guarded trophies, military assets and objects of study. Earlier generations of human soldiers and scholars have already learned to operate some examples and partially understand a few systems. Limited repairs, adaptations and cannibalisation are possible where knowledge and parts survive; reliable manufacture of their advanced cores, power sources and specialised components is beyond the current industrial base.

Psychic abilities and Warp phenomena are recognised features of life. Their interpretation varies between societies. Some communities place psykers under religious supervision; others employ them through scholarly institutions, hereditary service, or secretive circles of practitioners. Divination, warding, telekinesis, and ritual binding are studied with differing degrees of understanding and success.

Experiments combining machinery and psychic effects exist, usually within heavily guarded workshops or secluded sanctuaries. Such devices remain rare, temperamental, and dependent on particular practitioners. Records are fragmented, discoveries are closely guarded, and rival traditions frequently disagree over the causes of their successes and failures. Psychic practice offers real capabilities while carrying the dangers inherent in contact with the Warp.

The hunters are a regional spacefaring xenos species whose reach encompasses Malaspina and nearby systems. Their dominion is overwhelmingly vast to local human societies.

The alien hunters operate across all three continents. They arrive from offworld in advanced craft and deploy sophisticated armour, concealment systems, sensors, and weapons. Their expeditions target dangerous wildlife as well as humans. Decorated soldiers, experienced wilderness fighters, and powerful psykers can attract particular attention.

The humans experience their presence as a continuing war punctuated by raids, disappearances, ambushes, and destructive interventions. The hunters treat human resistance as part of the preserve’s value. Their activity varies between expeditions: some pursue individual prey, others attack military formations or interfere with particular installations.

They maintain sites in remote regions and respond aggressively to developments that threaten their access to the planet. Attempts to follow their craft with optical observation or experimental radio detection, approach their remote sites, or organise sustained attacks against their facilities have drawn retaliation. They would resist a future challenge to their offworld access; native humans have no established orbital programme. Their presence is intermittent and their surveillance imperfect, allowing human societies considerable freedom within a wider condition of confinement.

Malaspina is held in enforced technological stagnation. Human ingenuity produces advances, but recurrent hunter attacks destroy workshops, infrastructure, records and skilled communities, while recovery operations reclaim or destroy captured equipment. Concentrated alien arsenals, conspicuous use and threatening research attract particular attention. Losses repeatedly offset gains; permanent war preparations and death-world maintenance consume the resources needed for sustained development. Even without the hunters, survival would demand substantial labour. Raids are intermittent locally, but their cumulative pressure is planetary and persistent. Imperfect surveillance permits concealed caches and scattered expertise to survive.

The shared threat has produced alliances, mutual defence arrangements, and exchanges of military knowledge. It has also encouraged secrecy, appeasement, and accusations of collaboration. Rival states continue to compete for resources and territory, each convinced that control of its own institutions is essential to survival.

Malaspina remains a divided, inhabited world with substantial industrial resources, established cultures, decayed ruins, and vast territories beyond reliable human control. Its cities preserve wealth and learning behind formidable defences, while the wilderness and the skies remain contested.


Political geography. None of Malaspina’s three principal continents is unified. States hold defended settlements, cultivated districts and the transport corridors between them; large areas within their claimed borders remain beyond effective control. Hereditary claims, municipal privileges and commercial obligations frequently overlap.

Otranto contains the largest territorial powers and strongest mass armies. Morholt favours fortress leagues and dispersed forces adapted to forest and mountain terrain. Vesalius places greater weight on ports, coastal shipping and securing narrow routes inland. Overseas possessions and treaty ports are possible, but sustaining a large transcontinental empire would be exceptionally difficult.

The powers surrounding Serravonne form one regional political neighbourhood, not an exhaustive division of Vesalius or the planet.

March of Veyrasse. Veyrasse occupies part of Vesalius’s eastern coast: several fortified ports, cultivated coastal valleys and inland mining districts connected by railway. Serravonne is a secondary port and railway junction, strategically valuable but distant from the seat of government.

A hereditary margrave rules through a charter negotiated with landed houses, municipal councils and industrial proprietors. The margrave commands the standing army and conducts foreign relations. The other institutions supply much of the money, transport and manpower, giving them considerable influence over policy and mobilisation.

Engineers receive substantial professional respect, while education and patronage offer opportunities accompanied by obligations. Workshops, land and schooling are shaped by the interests of their patrons. Railway superintendent Leont Vardesca exercises authority over railway affairs and dependants; his office does not confer command of Serravonne’s government or army.

Duchy of Caldrienne. North of Veyrasse along the coast, Caldrienne is a more centralised hereditary state with productive agricultural valleys and substantial armaments works. Its army combines disciplined infantry, strong field artillery and the region’s greatest concentration of tanks.

Caldrienne is Veyrasse’s principal territorial rival. An armed truce restrains their dispute over the Cressault corridor, while trade continues across the frontier.

Karsenne Compact. Inland beyond the coastal escarpment, Karsenne is a federation of mining towns and fortress districts whose councils jealously protect their autonomy. Its military strengths are mountain troops, engineers, demolition specialists and fortified passes.

Karsenne is an essential supplier and uneasy defensive partner of Veyrasse. Its councils resent dependence on Veyrasse’s coastal export routes and are disputing freight charges. They seek an alternative route through Calvernis; Veyrasse regards that project as a threat to its revenues and strategic influence.

Calvernis Republic. South along the coast, Calvernis is governed through a restricted franchise dominated by competing shipping, banking and industrial families. Its military strengths include convoy escorts, coastal artillery, marines and propeller-driven maritime aircraft.

Calvernis is both a major commercial partner and a rival of Veyrasse, competing for railway contracts, harbour business and influence in smaller settlements. Its potential alternative export route for Karsenne adds to these tensions.

Ceralte Admiralty. Ceralte occupies an offshore island chain. A hereditary protector shares power with a council of senior naval officers and island governors. Experienced crews, torpedo craft, minelaying and operations in difficult coastal waters are its principal military strengths.

Ceralte is a treaty partner against piracy and hunter incursions. Mainland interests frequently accuse it of sheltering privateers targeting their commerce; accusation alone does not establish official complicity.

Trade and dependence. None of the regional powers can afford complete economic independence. Caldrienne purchases ores from Karsenne; Karsenne needs coastal ports; Veyrasse purchases machinery and shipping services from Calvernis; Ceralte imports grain. Political enemies remain customers and suppliers.

Disputes over Karsenne’s freight charges and alternative export route could end in negotiation, economic confrontation or armed conflict. No outcome has been settled.

Cressault corridor. The Cressault corridor is a chain of defended valleys near the Veyrasse–Caldrienne frontier, containing a railway crossing, irrigated farmland and the pumping infrastructure sustaining them. Both states claim it. Some estates owe hereditary obligations to one government while depending on the other for transport and protection.

A previous border war ended in an armistice without resolving ownership. Frontier troops exchange occasional fire and patrols sometimes fail to return. Customs inspections and railway delays serve as instruments of pressure. Both governments accuse the other of supporting armed local claimants and interfering with water supplies.

Renewed war is feared, but neither government has publicly committed to it. Serravonne is away from this front; its railway carries associated supplies and returning personnel.

Service and internal tensions. Conscription and industrial obligations create friction within Veyrasse. Wealthier households can secure favourable postings and protected occupations for their dependants. Poorer districts supply a disproportionate share of dangerous perimeter and field service.

Railway workers possess bargaining power because transport stoppages threaten both military operations and food deliveries. The station’s stores, schedules and workshops form part of the wider military economy. Staff maintaining civilian machinery also support military transport and defensive installations.

Veyrasse military institutions. The standing army fields infantry, artillery, engineers, a limited armoured force and supporting services. It fights other states and reinforces threatened districts.

Territorial garrisons combine local reservists with permanent cadres responsible for walls, alarms, evacuation routes, magazines and perimeter patrols.

Household forces belong to major estates and industrial patrons. Charters define their obligations to the state, but personal loyalties often remain strongest.

Serravonne defences. Serravonne maintains a permanent garrison, coastal gun positions, a mobilisation depot and railway-security personnel. Surrounding districts keep reserve rolls and emergency stores. Military engineers work on bridges, drainage systems and defensive clearings.

These institutions coexist with civilian railway administration. Control of transport, stores, military units and municipal affairs is divided rather than concentrated in the superintendent’s office.

Defence against the hunters. Against other human states, armies can pursue military victory and territorial settlements. Against hunter incursions, defenders commonly prioritise preserving communications, evacuating exposed people, denying easy approaches and concentrating conventional fire when an attacker becomes vulnerable.

Fortifications offer shelter and fighting positions, but cannot guarantee protection against attacks from above. Killing an isolated hunter is one form of success; preserving a reservoir or substantially reducing casualties can be another.

Warning agreements and limited mutual assistance connect rival states. Governments still dispute whether intervention will preserve a useful buffer, exhaust their reserves or expose their own forces to attack. Their shared enemy has not abolished competition.

Confirmed collaboration with hunters is rare and must be distinguished from widespread allegations. A settlement’s survival, a returning expedition or possession of a captured device may provoke suspicion without establishing collaboration.

Galahad’s regional knowledge. Through the first year’s books, railway conversation and local news, Galahad has learned the broad regional political geography, the states’ military specialisations and relationships, the Cressault dispute, and the significance of freight, food and mobilisation. This is contextual knowledge of the society around him.

Regional survey II. The eastern Vesalian atlas covers a region approximately 1,200 km east–west by 1,600 km north–south. Its local grid uses kilometres. Territorial shading records claims rather than continuous occupation. Caldrienne extends beyond the northern edge and Calvernis beyond the southern edge; their mapped areas are portions of larger states.

Veyrasse spans approximately 715 km north–south and up to 440 km east–west, with a charted claim of about 237,000 km². Karsenne’s charted claim covers about 263,000 km² across an elongated escarpment region about 1,000 km north–south. The Ceralte island chain shown spans about 915 km north–south and contains roughly 39,000 km² of charted island land; the intervening sea is not counted as land area. The disputed Cressault belt contains approximately 15,000 km² of overlapping claims and must not be counted as an additional sovereign state.

Auvrienne is Veyrasse’s seat of government, north of Serravonne. Valdrec is Caldrienne’s capital; Drossane hosts Karsenne’s councils; Miravelle is Calvernis’s government centre; Dalmor is Ceralte’s naval administrative seat on its principal central island. Serravonne remains a secondary fortified port and railway junction. The Ressant descends from the escarpment toward the Serravonne harbour area.

The main railway connects Serravonne northward to Auvrienne and the Cressault crossing, continuing into Caldrienne when the frontier route is open. An inland mountain line reaches Drossane, and a southern route reaches Miravelle. The alternative direct Karsenne–Calvernis alignment remains proposed, with no operational service. The atlas marks principal connections, not every local branch or settlement.

Serravonne to Auvrienne: approximately 350 km by rail, ordinarily 11–17 hours including normal stops and formalities.

Serravonne to Cressault crossing: approximately 575 km by rail, ordinarily 20–30 hours including normal stops and formalities.

Serravonne to Valdrec: approximately 805 km by rail, ordinarily 30–45 hours including normal stops and formalities.

Serravonne to Drossane: approximately 580 km by mountain rail, ordinarily 28–45 hours including normal stops and formalities.

Serravonne to Miravelle: approximately 475 km by rail, ordinarily 17–26 hours including normal stops and formalities.

Serravonne to Dalmor: approximately 270 km by sea, ordinarily 12–21 hours including normal stops and formalities.

These are practical passage estimates, not guaranteed schedules. Slow mountain running, border delays, weather, infrastructure damage and hunter activity can lengthen or suspend travel. Route kilometres follow the marked alignments rather than straight-line separation.

The Istravel Cut conservatory is 6 km from Serravonne by the upland railway branch. The first maintenance-trolley journey took 23 minutes. This branch is too short to distinguish on the regional sheet.
