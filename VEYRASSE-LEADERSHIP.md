# Veyrasse — court, command and noble standing

Public institutions and biographical reference. Ages, reign lengths and appointment durations below were recorded on 21/10/0067 AC43; birthdays and exact anniversaries are not established. Current personal dealings are recorded separately as of 11/10/0068.

## Ruling house
Margrave Odrienne Orcemont, woman, 50. Margrave is the sovereign office regardless of gender. Rules from the existing fortified palace/government precinct on Auvrienne's upper terraces. Reigned 18 years after her mother's death. Married; consort has household and ceremonial responsibilities, not joint sovereignty. Consort's name remains unestablished.
House Orcemont has held the margravial office for approximately 190 years, deriving its legitimacy from a charter settlement between frontier landholders, Auvrienne's municipal interests and coastal revenue holders. Not the royal house of all Vesalius. Survived the last major Hunter purge through continuity of government and provincial stores while losing family members, infrastructure and records. Its survival brought both legitimacy and resentment over unequal protection. Odrienne was born after that purge. Public governing record: defended charter prerogatives, pursued rail and defensive works, balanced creditors, municipalities and old houses. Reputation for controlled pragmatism, not affection for commoners. 
Heir: Lady Maurelle Orcemont, daughter, 25; eldest eligible recognised child. Undertakes treasury briefings, provincial visits and supervised council business. No independent army or sovereign authority. Younger brother, 21, next after her and her lawful descendants under ordinary succession. His name remains unestablished. Succession is birth-order without male preference among eligible recognised descendants; council verifies lawful succession, rather than freely electing a replacement. Regencies and disputed legitimacy require charter proceedings. Galahad's future succession remains subject to his own grant, not automatically every sovereign provision.

## Armed forces
Margrave is supreme commander and appoints senior command. Marshal Calvren Vaucerin, man, 62, is professional head of the March's armed forces. His remit is joint planning, mobilisation and army operations; naval service retains its own professional command under sovereign authority. Based in military secretariat adjoining palace, with front inspections. Appointed nine years ago; career artillery officer, then staff and field commands. Northern-border reputation rests on conserving trained troops, prepared positions, reliable artillery and rail supply; not an undefeated genius.
Vaucerin is an old minor service-noble family with a modest inland estate, serving through officers and officials rather than great commercial fortunes. Calvren is widowed, two adult children: daughter is a military doctor; son manages family property and accounts. Their names and ages remain unspecified. Neither inherits command. His family succession follows its grant, not military office.
Lieutenant-General Cevrel Darscelet, woman, 48, chief of general staff, is designated acting coordinator if he is incapacitated pending the Margrave's decision. Commission and promotion, not heredity; permanent replacement is not guaranteed. Known for mobilisation schedules and supply planning. Both have now privately heard Galahad’s report and examined his proposals. They support his royal technical commission and recommend future Royal Advisor appointment, subject to the sovereign’s review after produced rifle trials.

## Rank and law
Charter state with pronounced inherited inequality, not unrestricted feudal sovereignty. Noble standing applies to recognised family members of any gender. Lord/Lady and grant-specific Sir are styles, not universal officer commissions or knighthoods.
Benefits: entitled formal style and entry in noble register; eligibility for court presentation through procedure/sponsor, noble service appointments and household representation; protection against summary lower-official punishment, with serious charges involving nobles referred to charter court. Ordinary police can restrain immediate danger or arrest in flagrante. Bail and accommodation commonly favour rank in practice. Greater credibility, credit access and social networks; commoner testimony legally admissible, often weighed unequally in practice. No automatic court seat, tax exemption, stipend, troop levy, public judicial power, private prison or immunity. Estate rent follows existing leases and duties. Protected tenants retain rights. Household servants are paid people, not property. Fraud, bribery, murder and treason remain offences for nobles. Retainers' violence is not legally absolved by their master's rank.
A knowing unlawful blow to a noble can be charged as both assault and affront to chartered dignity. Injury, intent, weapons and circumstances determine sentence: fine, compensation, imprisonment or penal labour possible. No automatic execution for an ordinary strike. Accidental contact not affront. Necessary proportionate self-defence lawful regardless of rank, though witnesses, money and patronage affect whether it is recognised. Deliberate murder or grave attempted murder can carry death for any victim; attacking sovereign/official on duty can add state-security charges. Noble assaulting commoner still illegal, often handled more leniently in practice through compensation and influence. Nobles can suffer additional office/rank consequences for serious offences. Insult/disagreement alone not lawful grounds for killing. Private duelling does not create blanket immunity.
